01

Data and Information Management

Core focus: create reliable, timely and understandable information from institutional data.

Start with the basics

Data is not yet information

Data is a recorded fact, such as a mark of 62 or a payment date. Information is data organised in context—for example, a module pass-rate trend that helps a programme manager plan support.

Knowledge is the informed interpretation and action that follows, such as investigating why a cohort is struggling.

Typical campus data

Know what each system owns

  • Student management: identity, enrolment, programme and registration status.
  • Learning management: course access, submissions, attendance or activity and assessment results.
  • Finance: invoices, payments, balances, refunds and funding status.

Principle: one authoritative source per data item.

Agree which system is the official source for each field. Other systems can use a synchronised copy, but staff should know where corrections must be made. Avoid silently maintaining conflicting “master” records in several places.

A repeatable data-quality improvement cycle

01

Define the field and rule

Agree what “active student” means, who owns the definition and which system is authoritative.

02

Profile and measure

Count missing IDs, duplicate profiles, invalid codes and out-of-date synchronisations. Establish a baseline.

03

Correct at the source

Assign issues to authorised data owners. Validate changes and retain an audit trail; do not simply overwrite records in a report.

04

Prevent and monitor

Add validation, staff training and routine exception reports. Track the same measures monthly to check whether quality improves.

Dashboards and data visualisation

A dashboard should answer a specific management question—not merely display everything the system can count. Use a small number of well-defined measures, clear time periods, accessible labels and filters that match a real decision.

Make a chart useful

  • Use a line chart for change over time.
  • Use bars to compare programmes or faculties.
  • Start bar charts at zero and label units clearly.
  • Show the reporting period and last refresh time.
  • Do not rely on colour alone to carry meaning.

Ask before acting

  • Is the metric defined consistently?
  • Could a change reflect a late data feed?
  • Is the sample large enough to be meaningful?
  • Who owns the data and can verify it?
02

POPIA, Privacy and Ethics

Student records are personal information. Handle them lawfully, securely and for a clear institutional purpose.

What counts as personal information?

Names, student numbers, contact details, academic results, fee balances and identity numbers can all identify a person. Special care is needed for sensitive information, such as disability or health information, and for records about minors where relevant.

Apply appropriate safeguards

  • Limit access by role and review permissions regularly.
  • Set retention and secure disposal rules.
  • Keep audit logs and a documented incident-response process.
  • Use approved channels; do not email spreadsheets of student data unnecessarily.

Purpose and minimisation

Collect only what is needed for a defined institutional purpose. A lecturer may need a student’s enrolment and assessment status, but not necessarily their full fee history or unrelated personal details.

Ethics beyond legal compliance

Ask whether the use is fair, transparent, proportionate and respectful. Explain how information is used, reduce bias, provide ways to challenge errors and avoid turning a risk indicator into a label about a student.

When a privacy incident occurs

Contain the exposure if safe to do so, preserve relevant evidence, report it immediately through the institution’s incident and Information Officer channels, and follow the approved response process. POPIA has notification requirements for security compromises; staff should not independently decide to conceal, delay or publicly announce an incident.

This section is general educational guidance, not legal advice. The institution’s Information Officer and legal/privacy advisers should confirm its lawful basis, notices, contracts, cross-border transfers, retention periods and incident procedures.

03

AI and Decision Support

Use automation to help staff notice patterns—not to remove accountability from decisions affecting students.

Useful applications

  • Flag unusual changes in module pass rates for review.
  • Forecast support-service demand using appropriate aggregated data.
  • Help staff search approved policy documents.
  • Summarise operational trends for human review.
  • Identify duplicate or incomplete records for authorised staff to verify.

Know the difference

Business intelligence summarises what happened. A decision support system (DSS) helps compare options using data and models. AI may identify patterns or generate content, but can make errors or reproduce bias.

Risks to manage

  • Biased predictions affecting particular student groups.
  • False alerts or confident but incorrect generated answers.
  • Use of student data in unapproved external tools.
  • Opaque recommendations that cannot be explained.
  • Staff over-relying on a score instead of context.

Human-in-the-loop safeguards

  • Test performance across relevant groups before deployment.
  • Use the minimum necessary data and approved tools.
  • Tell users when AI contributes to an output.
  • Require trained human review for consequential decisions.
  • Provide a correction or appeal route and monitor outcomes.

Example: student-support alert

A model flags a student who may need academic support because submissions have declined. The alert should prompt a supportive check-in—not an automatic penalty, exclusion or assumption about motivation. Confirm data accuracy and allow the student to explain their circumstances.

04

Enterprise systems and integration

Connected systems can reduce duplicate work, but integration needs shared definitions, ownership and reliable technical controls.

Why integration breaks down

  • Different student identifiers or module codes.
  • Legacy systems with limited interfaces.
  • Different update schedules and data formats.
  • Manual spreadsheet transfers and re-keying.
  • Unclear ownership when data conflicts.
  • Vendor restrictions, cost and downtime risk.

Common integration approaches

  • API: systems exchange data through defined interfaces.
  • Middleware / integration platform: coordinates mapping, validation and transfers.
  • Scheduled batch: transfers a controlled set of records at intervals.
  • Reporting warehouse: combines governed copies for analysis without replacing operational systems.
Example data flowRule to agreeControl to implement
Student system → LMSWho is enrolled in which module, and when?Stable student/module IDs, validation, sync logs and exception queue.
Student system → FinanceWhich registration triggers a fee record?Reconciliation totals, duplicate prevention and controlled corrections.
All systems → DashboardWhat does “registered”, “active” or “at risk” mean?Data dictionary, refresh timestamp, access controls and lineage.

A safer integration roadmap

01

Map the current state

Document systems, owners, interfaces, critical fields, manual steps, costs and known failure points.

02

Agree governance and standards

Define the authoritative source, shared identifiers, formats, permitted uses, access roles and service expectations.

03

Pilot one high-value flow

Choose a contained use case, such as registration-to-LMS enrolment. Test success, duplicates, late changes, rejected records and recovery.

04

Reconcile, monitor and scale

Compare source and destination counts, alert on failures, document rollback and support procedures, then expand in stages.

Do not integrate bad data faster.

Resolve identity and data-definition problems before scaling automated transfers. Integration can propagate errors across every connected system if quality checks are missing.

05

Adapting systems to institutional needs

Fit systems to legitimate workflows while avoiding unnecessary complexity and expensive custom code.

Configure before customising

Start with supported settings: roles, approval steps, forms, notification rules, reports, terminology and validation. Configuration is usually easier to maintain than changing a vendor’s underlying software.

When custom development may be justified

Consider it when a critical legal, academic or operational need cannot be met safely through standard configuration or a supported integration. Assess cost, security, vendor upgrades and who will maintain it.

Design around real users

  • Interview lecturers, administrators, finance staff and students where appropriate.
  • Map the current process and identify avoidable steps.
  • Prototype with realistic but non-sensitive test data.
  • Test accessibility, mobile use, language and staff workload.
  • Train users and provide support after launch.

Change management matters

Explain why the change is happening, who is affected, what will change and where to get help. Appoint departmental champions, gather feedback and measure adoption instead of assuming installation equals success.
